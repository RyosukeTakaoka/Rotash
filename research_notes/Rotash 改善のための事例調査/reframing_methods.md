## 1. The "slow elevator / mirrors" story (Wedell-Wedellsborg) and his reframing method

### Takeaway
The elevator-mirror story is the opening example of Thomas Wedell-Wedellsborg's HBR article "Are You Solving the Right Problems?" (Jan–Feb 2017), and he expanded it in *What's Your Problem?* (HBR Press, 2020). He uses it to separate the *stated* problem ("the elevator is slow") from the *real* one ("waiting is annoying"). It is a teaching parable, not a documented case. Its origin is anecdotal, and he did not invent it.

### Cited Findings
- Setup as told in the HBR article: you own an office building and tenants complain the elevator is old and slow and they wait a lot. Several threaten to break their leases. Most people jump to "solution space" fixes: replace the lift, install a stronger motor, upgrade the algorithm — [search summary of HBR 2017 article and secondary write-ups; Medium/nyc-design](https://medium.com/nyc-design/reframing-the-problem-200f6c966dfc); [Katie Martin](https://katielmartin.com/2020/04/26/are-we-solving-the-right-problems/); [HBR podcast "The Secret to Better Problem Solving" (Dec 2016)](https://hbr.org/podcast/2016/12/the-secret-to-better-problem-solving)
- The reframe: building managers put up mirrors next to the elevator. The author stresses that this "doesn't make the elevator faster". It treats the problem as how the wait *feels*, because people lose track of time when they have something engaging to look at (here, themselves) — [same sources as above](https://medium.com/nyc-design/reframing-the-problem-200f6c966dfc)
- The key reframing question: "Is the speed of the elevator really the problem, or is there something else going on?" The author also says reframing "takes time and practice to get good at" — [Katie Martin summary](https://katielmartin.com/2020/04/26/are-we-solving-the-right-problems/)
- Retellings of the elevator-mirror origin (often set in a New York hotel or office building) are anecdotal. One source calls the mirror a "century-old marketing ploy", and none gives a dated, documented first case — [Elevator World blog](https://elevatorworld.com/blog/elevators-best-asset-and-illusion/); [Laughing Squid](https://laughingsquid.com/why-elevators-have-mirrors/)
- Book structure (*What's Your Problem?*, 2020): "Frame the Problem", then a reframing loop with chapters including "Look Outside the Frame", "Rethink the Goal" and "Examine Bright Spots". It ends with "Move Forward". The book separates "exploring the frame" (digging into the original problem statement) from "breaking the frame" (stepping away from it) — [book notes, vejdani.org](https://vejdani.org/books/wyproblem/); [Leadership Now review](https://www.leadershipnow.com/leadingblog/2020/03/whats_your_problem.html)
- The Reframing Canvas starts with a first framing of the problem and then runs a reframing loop. One check asks whether the problem statement is phrased neutrally or narrowly in a way that favors one solution — [dyanwilliamslaw.com summary](https://dyanwilliamslaw.com/2020/10/how-to-think-clearly-part2/)
- Free canvases, checklists and a primer are on the author's site howtoreframe.com — [vejdani.org](https://vejdani.org/books/wyproblem/)
- The same author's *Leader to Leader* piece (2020), "Facing a tough problem? Try reframing it", argues for looking at the whole situation, beyond the product, to find pain points nobody has noticed — [Wiley Online Library](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ltl.20506)
- **[Training knowledge, not re-verified]** The seven practices in the 2017 HBR article are: (1) establish legitimacy (get the group to accept that diagnosis matters), (2) bring outsiders into the discussion, (3) get people's definitions of the problem in writing, (4) ask what's missing, (5) consider multiple categories of problem, (6) analyze positive exceptions (bright spots), (7) question the objective. The 2020 book reorganizes these as Frame, then Reframe (Look outside the frame, Rethink the goal, Examine bright spots, Look in the mirror, Take their perspective), then Move forward.
- **[Training knowledge, not re-verified]** Earlier versions of the elevator parable appear in Russell Ackoff's writing on problem solving (e.g., *The Art of Problem Solving*, 1978), where a psychologist suggests mirrors. Wedell-Wedellsborg presents it as a classic story, not his own case.

### Inferences
- The method maps directly onto app feedback. A friend's "make the photos bigger" is the "slow elevator". Before building anything, ask what experience is actually bad (e.g., "I can't see my friends' faces", "the grid feels empty", "I don't know what to look at").
- The most portable steps for a student: write the problem down in one sentence, ask what's missing from that statement, try 2–3 different categories of problem (UI? motivation? social awkwardness? timing?), look for bright spots (the friend who *does* use it daily — why?), and question the goal (is the goal "more photos", or "feeling close to friends"?).
- Tell the story as an illustrative parable. Don't present it as a documented historical case.

### Gaps
- Could not open the HBR article or book to quote exact wording or confirm the seven-practice list. The list above is from training knowledge.
- No primary source confirms where or when mirrors were first used for this purpose. The anecdote is unverifiable.

## 2. Houston airport baggage claim (NYT 2012 "Why Waiting Is Torture"; David Maister)

### Takeaway
The story comes from a single NYT opinion piece (August 2012, "Why Waiting Is Torture"). The numbers (8-minute average wait, ~1-minute walk, ~7 minutes standing at the carousel, walk made ~6× longer, complaints fell to "near zero") all trace back to that one article. No airport records independently confirm them. It is a good illustration of Maister's principle that unoccupied time feels longer, but the exact figures should be cited as "as reported by the NYT".

### Cited Findings
- Houston airport officials got many complaints about baggage-claim waits. First fix: more baggage handlers, which cut the average wait to about 8 minutes, but complaints continued — [APS repost of NYT article](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/why-waiting-is-torture.html)
- Diagnosis: passengers walked about 1 minute from the gate to baggage claim and then spent about 7 more minutes standing at the carousel. Most of the wait was idle standing — [APS repost of NYT](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/why-waiting-is-torture.html)
- Fix: arrival gates moved farther from the main terminal and bags routed to the farthest carousel, so the walk became ~6× longer. Complaints dropped to "near zero" — [APS repost](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/news/why-waiting-is-torture.html); [Your Mileage May Vary fact-check post](https://yourmileagemayvary.com/2024/06/30/the-sneaky-way-this-texas-airport-stopped-passengers-complaints-about-long-waits/)
- The Your Mileage May Vary post, which fact-checks the viral meme, cites the same NYT account and no independent airport data — [Your Mileage May Vary](https://yourmileagemayvary.com/2021/09/20/the-sneaky-way-this-texas-airport-stopped-passengers-complaints-about-long-waits/)
- David Maister's "The Psychology of Waiting Lines" (1985) sets out eight propositions. They cover how unoccupied time, pre-process waits, anxiety, uncertainty, unexplained waits, unfair waits, the value of the service, and waiting alone vs. in a group each change how long a wait feels. Maister also notes anxiety is much higher while waiting to be served than while being served — [davidmaister.com](https://davidmaister.com/blog/201/The-Psychology-of-Waiting-Lines.html); [davidmaister.com articles](https://davidmaister.com/articles/5/52/index.html)
- Jones & Peppiatt (1996) experimentally tested two of Maister's propositions with 300 members of the public and proposed revisions, so the principles have some, but limited, empirical testing — [Emerald, Int'l Journal of Service Industry Management](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/09564239610149957/full/ris)
- **[Training knowledge, not re-verified]** The NYT author was Alex Stone, published Sunday Review, Aug. 18, 2012. Maister's first proposition is "Occupied time feels shorter than unoccupied time." The airport is usually identified as Houston's George Bush Intercontinental, but some retellings just say "Houston airport".

### Inferences
- Same lesson as the elevator story: the complaint ("bags are slow") named a symptom. The real problem was idle, uncertain waiting. Both fixes changed *perception*, not the measured metric.
- For a photo app: a complaint like "the app is slow" or "nothing happens" may really be about uncertainty (did my photo send? when will friends post?) or empty time. Progress indicators, previews and "X is shooting now" signals may help more than raw speed work.
- Ethical caveat worth noting for students: deliberately making people walk farther is manipulation that worked in this anecdote. In product terms, prefer honest fixes like occupying and informing users.

### Gaps
- Could not open the NYT article to quote verbatim or confirm the author and date. No airport source independently confirms the numbers.
- Some retellings say "six times longer". The arithmetic (1 min walk to ~6 min) is consistent but rests on the same single source.

## 3. Jobs to Be Done: the milkshake story, Moesta's switch interview and forces of progress, applying them to consumer social apps

### Takeaway
The milkshake story is real in outline. Bob Moesta (with Rick Pedi) did fieldwork for a fast-food chain and found that about 40% of milkshakes were bought early in the morning by lone commuters, who "hired" them for a long, boring drive. Some details vary or are embellished in retellings: the chain's name (often said to be McDonald's, unconfirmed), the percentage (40% vs. "50% before 8:30"), and claimed sales results ("sevenfold"). Moesta's switch interview and four forces (push, pull, anxiety, habit) give a concrete interview structure that applies to "why did/didn't my friend keep using the app".

### Cited Findings
- Core story: a fast-food chain's milkshake sales didn't improve after demographic segmentation and feedback-driven product tweaks. A researcher found that 40% of milkshakes were bought first thing in the morning by commuters ordering to go. Interviews showed they faced a long, boring commute, wanted something to occupy a hand, weren't hungry yet but knew they would be by 10 a.m. The thick shake through a thin straw lasted the commute and was tidy, unlike bagels, doughnuts or bananas — [HBS Working Knowledge "Clay Christensen's Milkshake Marketing" (2011)](https://www.library.hbs.edu/working-knowledge/clay-christensens-milkshake-marketing); [Chris Dixon, 2011](https://cdixon.org/2011/12/21/what-jobs-are-users-hiring-your-product-to-perform/)
- Christensen's own excerpt (*Competing Against Luck*, 2016): Bob Moesta and Rick Pedi, consultants from Detroit, were involved (mid-1990s). The morning customers shared a job, a long and boring ride to work, not a demographic trait. The same customers may "hire" a milkshake for a different job in the afternoon (e.g., a treat for a child) — [StartupNation book excerpt](https://startupnation.com/books/competing-against-luck-book-excerpt/)
- Method per a Christensen teaching note as quoted: the researcher spent a day watching purchases (time, alone or not, what else was bought, eaten on site or not). The next morning he interviewed customers leaving with shakes about which job they'd hired it for — [The TRIZ Journal](https://the-trizjournal.com/?p=15024)
- The competition is bagels, bananas and boredom, not other milkshakes — [PDMA review of Competing Against Luck](https://www.pdma.org/review_competing_aga)
- **Discrepancies and embellishments:** some summaries say "50% bought before 8:30 a.m." vs. 40% in the teaching note. Several secondary write-ups name McDonald's, but HBS and Christensen sources found don't name the chain. A "sales increased sevenfold" claim appears in a secondary account and was not confirmed in primary material — [search synthesis; JD Prater history](https://jdprater.substack.com/p/a-brief-history-of-jobs-to-be-done); [Yu-kai Chou critique](https://yukaichou.com/gamification-analysis/jobs-to-be-done-christensen-consumer-hiring/)
- The ReWired Group (Moesta's firm) has its own case page "Milkshakes in the Morning" (not opened) — [therewiredgroup.com](https://therewiredgroup.com/case-studies/milkshakes/)
- JTBD has rival schools. Tony Ulwick's Outcome-Driven Innovation treats jobs differently from the Christensen/Moesta "progress" view, and the history of who originated what is disputed — [Myles Innovation history](https://myles-innovation.com/blog/jtbd-history-christensen-ulwick/); [Roman Kir, Medium](https://medium.com/@roman-kir/above-the-frameworks-jtbd-how-a-brilliant-idea-turned-into-a-civil-war-30d60f727df5)
- Four forces of progress (Moesta/Spiek): **Push** of the current situation (struggle) and **Pull** of the new solution drive change. **Anxiety** about the new and **Habit** of the present hold it back. People switch when push + pull > anxiety + habit. Moesta notes pull and anxiety arrive together: the moment a new option becomes attractive, it also creates self-doubt — [JTBD Radio "Pull & Anxiety"](https://jobstobedone.org/radio/pull-and-anxiety/); [JTBD Radio "Unpacking the Progress Making Forces Diagram"](https://jobstobedone.org/radio/unpacking-the-progress-making-forces-diagram/); [Elemental Concept](https://elementalconcept.com/insights/moesta-four-forces-of-progress-jtbd/)
- Switch interview: reconstruct a real past decision as a timeline (first thought, passive looking, active looking, the deciding trigger, purchase/sign-up, first use, ongoing use). Ask where they were, who they were with, and what else was happening, not abstract opinions. Some practitioners start from the concrete sign-up or purchase moment and work backward — [Gavel guide](https://usegavel.com/bob-moesta/switch-interview); [Lenny's Podcast summary with Bob Moesta](https://podpulse.ai/podcast/lennys-podcast-product-growth-career/lennys-podcast-product-growth-career-the-ultimate-guide-to-jtbd-bob-moesta-co-creator-of-the-framework); [JTBD official site](https://jobstobedone.org/)
- One guide claims a 45-minute session spends ~20 minutes isolating the "first thought". That comes from a single, less authoritative source — [innovation-creativity.com](https://innovation-creativity.com/bob-moesta-switch-interview-45-minute-script-guide/)
- Des Traynor (Intercom) adopted and popularized JTBD for software. The idea is that features should follow the job, never lead it — [JTBD Radio: JTBD at Intercom](https://jobstobedone.org/?p=1816); [Antoine Buteau, lessons from Des Traynor](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-des-traynor/)

### Inferences
- **Applying JTBD to a social photo app:** the job is rarely "take a photo". More likely jobs are "feel connected to close friends without the pressure of performing", "have a lightweight reason to check in daily", "make a shared memory of this week". The competition isn't other photo apps. It's LINE/Instagram stories, group chats, or simply not bothering.
- **Switch interview adapted to friends who tested it:** "Think back to the first day you opened it. Where were you? Who invited you? What did you expect? When was the last time you opened it? What were you doing right before? Was there a day you meant to post and didn't? What happened?"
- **Forces for an app trial:** Push = what's unsatisfying about how they share photos now. Pull = what caught them. Anxiety = "what if my photo is boring / friends judge it / I forget my turn". Habit = they already post in a group chat. Low retention among friends often comes from habit and anxiety, not missing features.
- **"Firing" interviews** (why they stopped) are as useful as switch-in interviews for a tiny test group.

### Gaps
- Could not confirm the restaurant chain, the exact morning percentage, or any sales outcome from a primary source. **[Training knowledge, not re-verified]** Moesta has said in podcasts that the chain did not fully implement the recommendations. Treat any "sales rose X-fold" claim as unverified.
- No published, sourced JTBD case on a consumer social photo app was found in this search.

## 4. The Mom Test (Rob Fitzpatrick): rules, bad vs. good questions, compliments and feature requests

### Takeaway
The Mom Test's three rules: (1) talk about their life, not your idea; (2) ask about specifics in the past, not generics or opinions about the future; (3) talk less and listen more. It classes compliments, fluff (generic, hypothetical or future statements) and feature ideas as "bad data". Feature requests should be "understood, not obeyed": dig with "Why do you want that? What would that let you do?" Only commitments (time, reputation, money) count as real signal.

### Cited Findings
- Three rules: talk about their life instead of your idea; ask about specifics in the past instead of generics or opinions about the future; talk less and listen more — [mtlynch.io book report](https://mtlynch.io/book-reports/the-mom-test/); [Unusual VC summary](https://www.unusual.vc/rob-fitzpatricks-mom-test/); [Pushers: 3 rules with examples](https://wepushers.com/frameworks/the-mom-test/)
- Bad question example: "Would you pay X for a product which did Y?" People are overly optimistic about the future and want to make you happy. Better: ask what they currently do and spend, or actually ask for money — [mtlynch.io](https://mtlynch.io/book-reports/the-mom-test/); [Medium summary](https://medium.com/@poloniothais/book-summary-the-mom-test-by-rob-fitzpatrick-8440986cd92c)
- Hypothetical yeses are "fictional commitments". People say yes to hypothetical money all day — [Koji blog (2026)](https://www.koji.so/blog/mom-test-customer-interviews-2026)
- Good alternatives: "Talk me through the last time that happened", "What else have you tried?", "How much do you currently spend solving this (tools, contractors, your own time)?" — [Koji](https://www.koji.so/blog/mom-test-customer-interviews-2026); [Castan.ai question list](https://castan.ai/blog/customer-interview-questions)
- Three types of bad data: compliments, fluff and ideas/feature requests. Feature ideas are called the most dangerous — [HelloPM summary](https://hellopm.co/book-summary-the-mom-test-by-rob-fitzpatrick/); [Unusual VC](https://www.unusual.vc/rob-fitzpatricks-mom-test/)
- Compliments ("That sounds really interesting", "I love this!") cost the speaker nothing and are worthless as data. Deflect them and redirect to facts, e.g., ask about the workflow it would replace — [Unusual VC](https://www.unusual.vc/rob-fitzpatricks-mom-test/); [Wedgescout summary](https://wedgescout.com/blog/the-mom-test)
- Fluff ("I usually…", "I would…", "I might…") should be anchored to a specific instance: "Can you walk me through exactly what happened last Friday?" — [Bala Vishal, Medium](https://bala-vishal.medium.com/mastering-customer-conversations-the-mom-test-for-entrepreneurs-and-pms-23f7be4367ae); [Business Floss](https://www.businessfloss.com/books/the-mom-test)
- Feature requests: understand, don't obey. Dig with "Why do you want that? What would that let you do? Can you show me the last time you needed it?" Dig into emotions with "Tell me more about that." — [Wedgescout](https://wedgescout.com/blog/the-mom-test); [Field Notes by Nick](https://fieldnotesbynick.substack.com/p/the-mom-test-by-rob-fitzpatrick)
- Commitment and advancement: real signals cost the person time, reputation risk or cash (e.g., introductions to others, using it again, paying). Vague "keep me posted" is a polite no — [Unusual VC](https://www.unusual.vc/rob-fitzpatricks-mom-test/); [HelloPM](https://hellopm.co/book-summary-the-mom-test-by-rob-fitzpatrick/)
- Past behavior predicts future behavior better than stated intent. "Have you tried a solution, and how did it go?" beats "Would you use an app that…?" — [Medium summary](https://medium.com/@poloniothais/book-summary-the-mom-test-by-rob-fitzpatrick-8440986cd92c)
- The book is titled *The Mom Test: How to talk to customers & learn if your business is a good idea when everyone is lying to you* — [Goodreads](https://www.goodreads.com/book/show/52283963-the-mom-test)
- **[Training knowledge, not re-verified]** The book's name comes from the idea that even your mom, who loves you and will lie to protect your feelings, can't lie to you if you ask good questions. It's a direct analog to interviewing friends. Fitzpatrick's site is momtestbook.com. He also recommends keeping conversations casual (not formal "interviews"), deciding your "big 3" learning goals beforehand, and taking notes with symbols for pains, goals and obstacles.

### Inferences
- **Bad vs. good questions for a student's photo-app test:**
  - Bad: "Do you like the app?" / "Would you use it every day?" / "Would you use it if it had filters?" / "Is the grid layout good?"
  - Good: "When did you last open it? What made you open it?" / "Was there a day you didn't post? What were you doing instead?" / "When you got the notification that a friend posted, what did you do next?" / "How do you share photos with these friends today? When did you last do that?"
- **Handling "add X" from a friend:** say thanks, then ask "What would X let you do?" and "When did you last wish you had it?" Record the underlying need (e.g., "wanted to see who saw my photo" → need for reassurance or reaction), not the feature.
- Friends are the extreme case of "Mom". Assume positive statements are inflated and weight observed behavior (did they post on day 5 without being reminded?) far above opinions.

### Gaps
- Exact book quotes could not be verified. Wording above comes from secondary summaries that broadly agree with each other.

## 5. Other useful tools: 5 Whys, Levitt's "quarter-inch hole", Kano model, "feature requests are solutions", Superhuman PMF survey

### Takeaway
Each tool covers a different angle: 5 Whys for causal chains (with known limits), the drill/hole adage for outcome-over-feature thinking (its attribution is murky), Kano for sorting requests into basics vs. delighters, the "requests are solutions, find the problem" practice (Intercom; Shape Up), and the Sean Ellis/Superhuman "very disappointed" survey (40% benchmark). The PMF survey is only meaningful with a few dozen responses, not 3 friends.

### Cited Findings
**5 Whys**
- Taiichi Ohno's classic Toyota example: a welding robot stopped → fuse blew from overload → bearings not lubricated enough → oil pump not circulating enough → pump shaft worn → scrap got in because there was no strainer (filter). Root countermeasure: add a strainer — [ThinkReliability case study](https://thinkreliability.com/toyota-welding-robot-5-why/); [Lean Blog (Apr 2026), "Even Ohno's classic 5 Whys example deserves another why"](https://www.leanblog.org/2026/04/ohno-5-whys-actually-seven/)
- Limitations: "five" is arbitrary. Former Toyota employee Art Smalley reportedly said it was five because that's how many it took in that case. The 5th why is "a cause, not *the* cause". Single-chain analysis can miss multiple causal factors and often stops at "human error". Several critics sell competing root-cause tools, so weigh their critiques accordingly — [ThinkReliability](https://blog.thinkreliability.com/case-study-5-why-toyota-welding-robot); [TapRooT](https://taproot.com/?p=99165); [Scaler blog](https://scaler.com/blog/root-cause-analysis)
- Defense: the number is a guideline to dig below the surface. It can be applied several times along different branches — [No Jitter](https://www.nojitter.com/telecommunication-technology/the-five-whys-of-problem-solving)

**"People don't want a quarter-inch drill, they want a quarter-inch hole"**
- Quote Investigator: earliest strong match is a 1942 newspaper ad in Somerset, Pennsylvania (attributed to life-insurance agent C.C. Wagner). Percy Whiting's 1947 book popularized it and credited Leo McGivena (NY Daily News publicity manager). Theodore Levitt repeated it in *The Marketing Mode* (1969) crediting McGivena, which is why it is often misattributed to Levitt — [Quote Investigator (2019)](https://quoteinvestigator.com/2019/03/23/drill/); [Barry Popik](https://www.barrypopik.com/index.php/new_york_city/entry/dont_sell_drills_sell_holes)
- Product-manager counterpoint: the customer may not even want the hole. They want the shelf up, or the pride of a tidy room, so keep asking "and then what?" — [Dean on Delivery](https://www.deanondelivery.com/product_management/a-product-managers-response-to-the-story-of-the-%C2%BC-drill-bit/)

**Kano model**
- Noriaki Kano, 1984 (Tokyo University of Science). Categories: Must-be (absence causes strong dissatisfaction, presence adds little), Performance/One-dimensional (satisfaction scales with quality), Attractive/Delighter (delights when present, no penalty when absent), Indifferent, Reverse (some users dislike it) — [Microsoft Research "KANO Best Practices" PDF](https://microsoft.com/en-us/research/uploads/prod/2022/01/KANO-Best-Practices.pdf); [Agile Brand Guide](https://agilebrandguide.com/wiki/models/kano-model/)
- Delighters decay into performance and then must-be attributes as users get used to them and competitors copy. This is widely repeated but the timing is illustrative, not well measured — [Checkbox (Mar 2026)](https://www.checkbox.com/blog/kano-model); [Think Insights](https://thinkinsights.net/consulting/kano-model)
- **[Training knowledge, not re-verified]** The Kano questionnaire asks each feature as a functional/dysfunctional pair ("How would you feel if the app had X?" / "…did not have X?") with five answer options, and needs a reasonable sample to classify features.

**Feature requests are solutions: find the problem**
- An Intercom blog article, "Start with the problem to achieve better solutions" (attributed by an aggregator to Stephen Forbes), argues every feature request is a proposed solution and the team must find the problem behind it — [echai.ventures Intercom index](https://echai.ventures/startingup/from/intercom) (attribution secondhand)
- Des Traynor's "Product strategy means saying no": adding a feature for one loud customer quietly costs every other user. His filters ask whether most users will engage with it, whether it makes money, and whether it can be maintained — [Antoine Buteau, lessons from Des Traynor](https://www.antoinebuteau.com/lessons-from-des-traynor/); [Business of Software recap](https://businessofsoftware.org/2015/01/google-hangout-des-traynor-recap-highlights/)
- Shape Up (Basecamp, Ryan Singer): no backlog. Raw ideas are "shaped" into bounded pitches and bet on at a betting table for 6-week cycles. Book free at basecamp.com/shapeup — [Rework summary](https://resources.rework.com/libraries/project-management/shape-up-method); [Signal v. Noise](https://signalvnoise.com/svn3/shape-up-with-ryan-singer/); [Pendo Product Love podcast with Singer](https://www.pendo.io/pendo-blog/product-love-podcast-ryan-singer-head-of-strategy-at-basecamp-and-author-of-shape-up/)
- **[Training knowledge, not re-verified]** Shape Up's "Bets, Not Backlogs" chapter says Basecamp doesn't keep a central backlog of requests. Requests are tracked informally by whoever hears them (support, sales), and an important idea "will come back". Shaping starts by asking what the request is really about: "What's the problem? Why does it matter?"

**Superhuman / Sean Ellis product-market-fit survey**
- Question: "How would you feel if you could no longer use [product]?" (Very / Somewhat / Not disappointed). Sean Ellis benchmark: if more than 40% say "very disappointed", the product likely has PMF. The benchmark comes from comparing ~100+ startups (one source says "over 150 companies") — [Founders Network](https://foundersnetwork.com/superhuman-product-market-fit/); [Business of Software talk by Rahul Vohra](https://businessofsoftware.org/talks/product-market-fit-engine/)
- Superhuman's first score (2017) was 22% "very disappointed". Vohra surveyed users who had been using the product for a while, about 21 days in per one source — [First Round Review article via 0-to-traction mirror](https://0-to-traction.com/articles/review.firstround.com/superhuman-product-market-fit-engine); [Founders Network](https://foundersnetwork.com/superhuman-product-market-fit/)
- The engine: segment to the "very disappointed" group to define the high-expectation customer, politely disregard "not disappointed" users, focus on "somewhat disappointed" users whose main benefit matches the core fans, and split the roadmap ~50/50 between doubling down on what fans love and fixing what holds back the on-the-fence users — [Postulate summary](https://postulate.us/@samsonzhang/frameworks/p/2021-04-15-How-Superhuman-Used-a-Four-Question-oGqEg8zUriGRekTfnBUHwD); [0-to-traction mirror](https://0-to-traction.com/articles/review.firstround.com/superhuman-product-market-fit-engine)
- The score was reported to rise from 22% to 58% in under a year (podcast summary; verify against First Round original) — [SaaS Club podcast](https://saasclub.io/podcast/rahul-vohra-superhuman-342/)
- **[Training knowledge, not re-verified]** The four survey questions: (1) How would you feel if you could no longer use Superhuman? (2) What type of people do you think would most benefit from Superhuman? (3) What is the main benefit you receive from Superhuman? (4) How can we improve Superhuman for you? Ellis suggests surveying people who have experienced the core product recently (e.g., used it at least twice, within the last two weeks). Vohra noted ~40 responses is enough to start seeing directional results.

### Inferences
- For the friends' feedback, use 5 Whys lightly ("why does that matter to you?" 2–4 times) but branch. One complaint can have several causes (UX, social awkwardness, notification timing).
- Kano helps sort requests: "my photo must not get lost" = must-be; "faster upload" = performance; "a surprise weekly collage" = delighter. Don't build delighters while must-bes are broken.
- Ask the PMF question of the friends anyway, as a conversation opener ("How would you feel if this app disappeared tomorrow?"), followed by "Why?". With 3 people, though, it's a qualitative probe, not a metric.

## 6. Judging feedback from a tiny sample (3 friends) vs. behavior data, and the pitfalls

### Takeaway
Three friends can reveal usability problems and generate hypotheses about the "real problem". They can't validate demand, predict retention or rank features. Their stated opinions are biased: friends are polite, people predict their future behavior poorly, and preferences reported before real use are unreliable. Weight what they did (opens, posts, drop-off day, unprompted invites) far more than what they say, and turn each stated request into a hypothesis to check against behavior or a bigger sample.

### Cited Findings
- Jakob Nielsen's "First Rule of Usability? Don't Listen to Users" (2001): watch what users actually do when trying tasks. Self-reported claims are unreliable, and so are users' speculations about their future behavior. Gather preference data only after users have used a design and have a real feel for it — [blueskyonmars repost of Alertbox 2001](https://www.blueskyonmars.com/2001/08/13/first-rule-of-usability-dont-listen-to-users); [downes.ca](https://downes.ca/post/2684); [NN/g video: User Feedback vs. Innovation](https://www.nngroup.com/videos/user-feedback-vs-innovation/)
- Nielsen's later clarification: he doesn't advocate ignoring customers, but "watch what they do rather than listen to what they say". He calls this revealed preferences vs. the "query effect", where asking a question creates an opinion the person didn't have before — [Jakob Nielsen Substack, "Say vs. Do"](https://jakobnielsenphd.substack.com/p/say-vs-do); [UX Tigers](https://uxtigers.com/post/say-vs-do)
- Jeff Atwood (Coding Horror) on the same principle: blindly following user requests and feedback will likely lead to trouble — [Coding Horror](https://blog.codinghorror.com/i-repeat-do-not-listen-to-your-users/)
- The Mom Test: people want to make you happy and are overly optimistic about future behavior, so hypothetical "would you" answers are unreliable. Friends and family are the extreme case — [mtlynch.io](https://mtlynch.io/book-reports/the-mom-test/); [Unusual VC](https://www.unusual.vc/rob-fitzpatricks-mom-test/)
- Superhuman/Ellis PMF scoring explicitly segments respondents and ignores users who wouldn't be disappointed, meaning signal depends on having enough respondents to segment — [0-to-traction mirror of First Round](https://0-to-traction.com/articles/review.firstround.com/superhuman-product-market-fit-engine)
- JTBD research emphasizes reconstructing actual past behavior and context (where, when, with whom) over opinions — [Gavel switch-interview guide](https://usegavel.com/bob-moesta/switch-interview)
- **[Training knowledge, not re-verified]** Nielsen's "5 users find ~85% of usability problems" (2000) applies to *usability* testing of a single design with a homogeneous user group. It does not apply to estimating demand, satisfaction or feature priority. So 3 testers can catch obvious friction but not answer "will people keep using this?"

### Inferences
- **Practical triage for a student with 3 tester friends:**
  1. **Separate the statement from the evidence.** Log each piece of feedback as: *Stated request* → *Underlying problem hypothesis* → *Evidence from behavior* → *Next test*. E.g., "Add filters" → "worried my photo looks boring next to others (anxiety)" → "skipped posting on 2 of 7 days" → "test showing friends' photos only after you post / a 'no-judgment' framing".
  2. **Trust convergence, not counts.** If 2 of 3 *independently* hit the same moment of friction while you watch, it's probably real. If one friend asks for a feature, it's a lead to dig into, not a requirement.
  3. **Behavior beats opinion.** Did they open it without a nudge? On which day did they stop? Did they invite anyone? (Mom Test "advancement".) Look at analytics where available.
  4. **Discount predictions.** "I'll probably get bored of it" or "I'd use it every day" are forecasts and are unreliable either way. Ask about the last time they got bored of a similar app and what happened.
  5. **Watch out for the friend effect.** They'll hide negatives and inflate positives. Ask them to show you (screen share or hand you the phone) and ask "what was confusing" rather than "did you like it".
  6. **Don't let the loudest friend set the roadmap** (Traynor's point about one loud customer).
  7. **Reframe before solving.** For each top complaint, write 2–3 alternative problem statements (elevator method) and look for a bright spot (the friend who kept using it — why?).
- When the sample grows (20–40+ real users), add the Ellis/Superhuman survey and Kano questionnaires as quantitative checks.

### Gaps
- No rigorous source was found that gives a minimum sample size for qualitative discovery interviews of this kind. Practitioner advice varies.
- No peer-reviewed study was found quantifying the "friends are polite" bias in product feedback specifically. The claim rests on practitioner books (Mom Test) and general self-report research (Nielsen).
